# Iron Carbonate — Oxidizing Siderite Off Calcite

Practical sequence for using hydrogen peroxide to break down siderite (FeCO₃) while sparing calcite (CaCO₃).

1. **Pick your concentration.** Standard 3% drugstore peroxide is the safe starting point and works fine for most specimens, just slower. 6–12% (available as hair-developer grade) speeds things up but is more aggressive and needs gloves and eye protection — it can bleach skin and irritate eyes/lungs in a poorly ventilated space.

2. **Test on an inconspicuous spot first.** Even though calcite shouldn't react with peroxide, some specimens have other minerals mixed in (pyrite, other sulfides, certain clays) that can react unpredictably or even generate heat. A small test patch for 15–20 minutes tells you what you're dealing with before committing the whole piece.

3. **Submerge or apply the peroxide.** For a full specimen, soak it in a glass or plastic container (not metal — peroxide reacts with metals) with enough peroxide to cover the siderite areas. For spot treatment, soak a cotton pad or paper towel in peroxide and lay it directly over the siderite, keeping it wet.

4. **Let it work and refresh often.** Peroxide decomposes fairly quickly, especially once it starts reacting with the iron, so it loses potency within hours. Check every few hours and swap in fresh peroxide; a stubborn siderite crust often needs multiple soak cycles over one to several days rather than a single long soak.

5. **Watch for the color change.** As Fe²⁺ oxidizes you should see the siderite turn rusty orange-brown (iron oxide/hydroxide) and become chalky or friable — that's the sign it's breaking down structurally rather than just discoloring.

6. **Mechanically remove the loosened oxide.** Once it's punky, a soft brush (toothbrush for robust specimens, a finer brush for delicate crystal faces), gentle water rinsing, or careful use of a pick can lift the crumbly rust layer away. Don't scrub calcite faces hard since they scratch easily (calcite is only hardness 3).

7. **Repeat as needed.** Alternate soak-and-brush cycles until the siderite is gone or reduced to where you want it.

8. **Optional oxalic acid follow-up.** If a stubborn rust stain remains after the peroxide has done the structural work, a dilute oxalic acid soak (a teaspoon or two per cup of hot water is a common ratio) will chelate the remaining iron oxide into soluble ferric oxalate and lift the last of the staining. Keep exposure short and rinse thoroughly afterward, since oxalic acid is still a mild acid and prolonged contact can dull calcite's luster.

9. **Final rinse and dry.** Rinse thoroughly in distilled water to stop any residual reaction and remove dissolved iron/oxalate before air-drying.

## Cautions

Work in a ventilated area, don't mix peroxide with other cleaning chemicals (especially anything acidic at the same time — mix sequentially, not together), and avoid unnecessary heat, which speeds the reaction but also raises the risk of thermal stress cracking a specimen with mixed minerals of different expansion rates.
